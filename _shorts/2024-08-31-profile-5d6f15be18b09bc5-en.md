<p>I have always known that I have two sides: more specifically, a combination of PM (project manager) and RD (research and development engineer) traits.</p>

<p>While growing up, those sides sometimes pulled me into internal conflict. As I gradually learned to manage the associated feelings and abilities, they became part of everyday life instead.</p>

<p>That everyday change has shaped who I am today.</p>

<p>Most concretely, my life feels like a continuous stream of projects, with me operating independently like a one-person company.</p>

<p>By bringing projects into use, I can lead by example and help students experience the satisfaction of completing something people can actually use.</p>

<p>This dual identity lets me keep an overview at every stage, ensure technical feasibility, manage time and resources, and move projects toward implementation. It also keeps me thinking and moving forward.</p>

<p>Moving naturally between two roles.</p>

<p>As a PM, I need to imagine market possibilities, create ways to close the gap between expectations and reality, and understand overall progress. As an RD engineer, I need to work through technical details and solve development problems.</p>

<p>I often switch freely between these roles to find the best approach at each stage.</p>

<p>Whenever an idea begins to develop into a possible new project,</p>

<p>I first establish a clear vision based on market needs and technical feasibility.</p>

<p>When guiding students, I usually connect the full workflow in my own development environment first to establish feasibility, then help them implement a proof of concept (POC).</p>

<p>This verifies the concept and lays the foundation for later product development.</p>

<p>Once the POC succeeds, I define specifications for a minimum viable product (MVP) based on market feedback and user needs so it can address demand and offer commercial value.</p>

<p>These first two steps—the journey from zero to one—are the stages I enjoy most.</p>

<p>Finally, I look for market-entry opportunities and work with the engineering team on restructuring the system so the MVP can become a SaaS service supporting tens of thousands of simultaneous users. This is the journey from one to one hundred. At that point, clear specifications make it possible to estimate development resources and plan staffing through scenarios.</p>

<p>Sensitivity to system analysis and decision-making.</p>

<p>In project management, I pay particular attention to system analysis and the timing and sequence of decisions.</p>

<p>Whenever I plan a project, I analyze the system in detail first so each step has a clear technical path and solution.</p>

<p>This preparation helps reduce risk and keeps work moving smoothly.</p>

<p>Enabling communication and collaboration across departments.</p>

<p>Combining PM and RD roles often makes me a communication bridge.</p>

<p>I need to speak technical language with engineers and business language with a CEO.</p>

<p>That dual perspective helps departments collaborate on a shared basis.</p>

<p>Personal style and project management.</p>

<p>I often feel those two sides cooperating in my work.</p>

<p>Intuition helps me identify essential ideas and a project blueprint early, while rational analysis helps turn those ideas into a concrete product.</p>

<p>I try to balance them, respecting intuitive creativity without letting analysis constrain its development.</p>

<p>As a PM and RD engineer, I am attentive to emotional management: maintaining focus under pressure and adjusting how I work so project progress and team morale can move forward together.</p>

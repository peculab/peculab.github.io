<p>When designing the nationwide AI Python hands-on course for senior high and vocational high school students, I have always believed that the key to learning programming is not simply whether students have taken an introductory course. It is whether they are curious about technology and willing to spend time building things.</p>

<p>Even without prior experience, students with sufficient interest and motivation can learn AI technology through practice. After teaching the course last semester, however, I also found that this prerequisite is a real challenge for many high school students.</p>

<p>Many students already carry a heavy academic workload. Finding additional time to explore new technology is not easy. Those who truly enjoy and benefit from the course therefore tend to be students with a strong interest in technology who actively make time for it, rather than those trying it because “AI is popular” and they are afraid of falling behind.</p>

<p>On whether this course is suitable for first-year high school students, my position has always been this: prior programming experience is not necessarily required, but motivation for self-directed learning is.</p>

<p>If students join only because of a trend and are unwilling to spend time practicing, their learning will remain limited even if they have taken an introductory programming course.</p>

<p>Conversely, students who are curious about technology and willing to explore can learn to build AI applications through this course, even without formal programming experience. I hope to attract students who care about the field and want to explore it, rather than turn learning into pressure or a course requirement completed only on paper.</p>

<p>Understanding classroom teachers' concerns: introductory courses matter.</p>

<p>I also understand the concerns of teachers on the front line. From their perspective, replacing an existing introductory AI course with this hands-on course could deprive students of an opportunity to learn basic concepts.</p>

<p>They worry that without that foundation, students may struggle to keep up with AI applications or become frustrated with programming. They would therefore like students to develop basic AI literacy through an introductory course before taking AI Python practice. That is indeed a more gradual learning path.</p>

<p>I simply want to offer another possibility: learning programming logic while solving problems through hands-on work, without necessarily waiting until every foundational topic has been mastered before exploring AI.</p>

<p>These approaches each have strengths and suit different learners.</p>

<p>Ultimately, I believe the key is not just whether students have taken a particular foundational course, but whether they have the curiosity and ability to learn actively.</p>

<p>My course is not intended to replace introductory AI literacy courses entirely. It offers an opportunity better suited to students with strong interests and a high degree of self-directed learning.</p>

<p>I hope the two approaches can complement each other rather than compete.</p>

<p>If a teacher is convinced that students at their school need an introductory AI course to transition successfully into mine, I absolutely support that teacher in asking the school to restore the original course.</p>

<p>I do not want my course to become a source of resentment for other teachers, and I certainly do not want anyone committed to education to feel that their work is being displaced.</p>

<p>Our shared goal should be for students to learn, rather than to argue about which course is better. The ideal outcome is to plan the learning path that best meets their needs.</p>

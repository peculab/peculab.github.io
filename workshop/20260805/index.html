AI Future Lab Summer 2026 · August 3–7

Learning engineering from nature.

During the August 5 University of Washington lab visit, high-school students saw more than tiny aircraft and robots. They saw how researchers observe nature, combine disciplines, test imperfect ideas, and turn small discoveries into useful technologies.

Research lessonNature as engineer

Biomimicry begins with understanding—not copying a shape.

Nature solved flight, turning, balance, and sensing long before people built machines. The research process asks how an insect accomplishes complex work with very little mass, then translates those principles through mechanics, electronics, materials, programming, and repeated experiments.

The Coin-Copter illustrates the challenge. At approximately one gram, every component must be small and light while still producing enough thrust and stability. Foldable propellers reduce its footprint, then deploy for flight. Mechanical damping reduces oscillation, while software adjusts the main and tail motors to control direction.

Student learningThree lasting ideas

Observe, connect, and keep trying.

  • Learn to observe again. A wing, an insect's movement, or an everyday adaptation can contain a research question.
  • Connect disciplines. Biology, physics, mechanics, electronics, materials, and computing meet in one functioning system.
  • Treat iteration as evidence. Parts and test rigs record failed attempts, new questions, and the next modification—not disorder.

Co-supervisor perspective: science is more than formulas, and code is more than text on a screen. Together, different kinds of knowledge can make an idea fly or help a fingertip-sized machine begin to see and move.

My role throughout all five days

Co-supervising the learning environment—not only accompanying one tour.

As PecuLab founder and co-supervisor, I was present each day. Professor Shih-Chieh Hsu led curriculum design, research content, and academic-resource connections. I focused on whether students genuinely participated, crossed cultural boundaries, found a place in their teams, and began moving from learner toward contributor.

Cross-border cohortTaiwan · multiple U.S. states

International participation requires more than putting students in one room.

Students brought different ways of learning, speaking, asking questions, and joining groups. Some preferred to think fully before speaking; others shared ideas while they were still forming. My role was to help students recognize these differences as team resources, while ensuring that language, confidence, and educational habits did not quietly determine who participated.

Leadership pathwayDoctoral students → high-school students → younger learners

Education becomes sustainable when a learner's role begins to move.

Doctoral students taught high-school students; local high-school assistants supported peers; and the previous week, Junior NATEA high-school leaders had guided middle-school students. Together, these experiences formed a connected development pathway rather than isolated camps.

What we were developing: young people who learn technology while also learning to understand themselves, work with others, assume responsibility, and use their abilities to help someone else move forward.

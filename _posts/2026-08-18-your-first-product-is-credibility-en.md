This article is for young people who have many startup ideas but do not know what their first step should be.

It is also for those looking for funding, connections, cofounders, or admission to an accelerator before they have found their first paying customer.

A few days ago, an undergraduate at the University of Washington contacted me after seeing my LinkedIn profile.

He politely asked whether I would be willing to hear about the problems he was facing and offer some guidance based on my experience.

I said yes.

## What Qualifies Me to Advise Him?

By a somewhat unserious calculation, I started entrepreneurship at the age of three, when I began following my parents through their entrepreneurial lives. However many years my father has been an entrepreneur, I can jokingly claim about the same number of years of experience.

Of course, I would not call myself a successful entrepreneur. Nor do I believe that being older or having seen more automatically makes someone right.

But for more than forty years, I have done many things that other people might describe as lacking a proper career. I have observed different businesses, met all kinds of people, and personally experienced entrepreneurship, partnerships, poor judgment, and starting over.

Those experiences did not give me a formula that guarantees success. They did gradually teach me which holes are not worth falling into, which costs can be avoided, and which attractive opportunities are merely well packaged traps.

I cannot tell a young person what will guarantee success.

I can point out which activities are unlikely to move him closer to it, and which unanswered questions may later require far more time and money to resolve.

If I have accumulated anything over these forty years, it is probably not a résumé everyone envies or a model of success that others can simply copy.

It is the ability to avoid some unnecessary mistakes and, through a long series of choices, build a relatively free life for myself.

That is why I was willing to hear his concerns.

Not because I know every answer, but because I have already traveled some of the roads that lead nowhere.

## Can You Name 20 People Who Might Pay?

He told me that he had many excellent AI startup ideas. He simply did not know how to take the first step.

The question sounded simple, but it contained several misconceptions common among young founders.

I did not begin by asking which model he wanted to use, who would write the code, or how quickly he could produce a prototype.

I asked him:

> Can you name 20 specific people who are most likely to use this product and perhaps even pay for it?

If the product is intended to be free, then another question follows:

> How many free users would you need, and through what mechanism would they eventually generate paying customers or another form of revenue?

If you cannot answer either question, you may not yet have a startup opportunity. You may only have an idea for a product.

The existence of a market problem does not mean the market will choose you to solve it.

People saying that your product is cool does not mean anyone will pay for it.

The first step in entrepreneurship is not building a product.

It is identifying exactly whose problem you are solving and whether that problem is valuable enough to solve.

## A Market Problem Does Not Mean the Market Will Choose You

He then told me that he wanted to build a matching platform that would make it easier for Taiwanese brands to enter the United States and for American brands to enter Taiwan.

So I asked him:

> If you are not currently part of any brand's decision making circle, and you have no record in cross border distribution, regulation, logistics, or sales, why would these brands entrust their entry into another country to an undergraduate who has not yet graduated?

The question sounds harsh, but it is not a rejection of young people.

The real question is: Where will your credibility come from?

A Taiwanese brand entering the United States may need help with positioning, regulation, tax, logistics, distribution, pricing, local marketing, and customer service. An American brand entering Taiwan will face a similar set of challenges in the opposite direction.

Therefore, “cross border expansion is difficult” is a market observation, not a business model.

“I want to help these brands” is a motivation, not a competitive advantage.

A founder must answer:

1. Which side of the brand market do you actually have access to?
2. Which distributors or consumers on the other side can you reach?
3. Which costs or risks can you reduce?
4. Which revenue or opportunities can you add?
5. Why should a brand choose you instead of a consulting firm, agency, or established distributor?
6. What will you accumulate from one transaction that makes the next transaction easier?

## Building a Platform Does Not Automatically Create a Platform

Platform startups are particularly vulnerable to one misconception.

Many founders assume that once they build the website, buyers and sellers will appear automatically.

But building a platform does not automatically create a functioning platform.

When neither side has users, brands see no distribution, while distributors see no brands worth working with. The founder is left with a complete and attractive website that no one uses.

Real platforms often begin with highly manual services that do not appear scalable at all.

I would not advise this student to begin by building a platform for every brand moving between Taiwan and the United States. The scope is far too broad.

I would ask him to choose one direction, one product category, one city, one specific problem, and one measurable outcome.

For example:

> Help three Taiwanese food brands test entry into five Asian supermarkets in the Seattle area.

Or:

> Help five independent American lifestyle brands find their first distributors in Taiwan.

Do not begin by serving both countries, every industry, and every brand.

Complete the first few matches personally. Write the emails, make the visits, compile the lists, arrange the meetings, clarify the requirements, and even help complete the first small transaction.

Only through this process will the founder discover where brands actually get stuck. Is the problem finding distributors, understanding regulations, setting prices, doing local marketing, or knowing whom to trust?

After repeating the same work several times, you will begin to see which processes can be standardized, which data are worth accumulating, and which steps genuinely require software.

Until then, you are not operating a platform. You are providing a manual service.

There is nothing shameful about that. In fact, it may be the most reliable way to begin building a platform.

## Investors Do Not Owe You Money Because Your Idea Moves You

He later asked:

> Other than university programs and American accelerators, are there no other ways to raise money?

Of course there are.

But this question also contains a common misconception. Many people treat not having money as the main reason they cannot begin.

Investors and venture capital firms are not charities.

An investor buys part of a company today because the investor believes that ownership may become dramatically more valuable in the future.

Early investments fail at a high rate. The few companies that succeed must therefore have the potential to grow tens or even hundreds of times to support the overall portfolio.

The fact that your own idea moves you does not mean someone else should give you money.

Investors want to know:

1. Who needs this product?
2. How serious is the problem?
3. Who is willing to pay?
4. How many people are already using it?
5. Why do customers stay?
6. What does it cost to acquire a customer?
7. Can the model be repeated?
8. Is the market large enough to support a rapidly growing company?
9. Even if others recognize the opportunity, why are you the one who can win?

An idea alone is rarely an investable asset.

A company that can repeatedly acquire customers, generate revenue, and potentially scale is.

## Your First Capital Does Not Have to Come From Investors

Startup circles often describe the earliest sources of capital as the three Fs: Friends, Family, and Fools.

These are friends, relatives, and people willing to bet on a founder before much evidence exists.

The phrase is a joke, but it reflects a reality. When a company has no product, revenue, or growth data, people are often investing in their trust in the founder rather than in business evidence.

If you do not have a wealthy family, you may need partners willing to share the risk. You may also need to build initial capital through work, consulting, and early revenue.

But do not confuse someone believing your story with the market proving that you have a business.

Several blockchain projects in recent years had no clear application, yet still raised substantial amounts by issuing tokens and generating hype and speculative demand.

That shows that stories, attention, and market emotion can temporarily create a price. It does not show that they create enduring value.

If a venture can survive only through information asymmetry, speculation, or the hope that someone else will buy at a higher price, it has not proven a business model. It has only proven an ability to exploit less informed participants.

For most young founders, the best first capital may not come from investors.

It may come from the first paying customer, an early user willing to preorder, a brand willing to participate in a pilot, revenue from consulting or managed services, a competition or research grant, or savings accumulated through work and freelance projects.

Investor money can accelerate a model that has received some initial validation. It rarely creates demand that did not exist.

If an idea requires a large amount of money before you can discover whether anyone needs it, the idea probably has not yet been broken into small, testable questions.

## A Four Week Experiment That Requires No Fundraising

For this Taiwan and United States brand matching idea, I would recommend a four week experiment that requires no outside capital.

### Week One: Narrow the Market

Choose one category, one city, and one direction, such as helping Taiwanese food brands enter Seattle.

### Week Two: Interview the Market

Interview ten Taiwanese brands and ten American distribution decision makers.

Do not pitch the platform yet. Learn how they currently handle cross border expansion, where they most often fail, and what one failure costs them.

### Week Three: Provide a Manual Service

Select the most concrete problem from the interviews. It might be identifying suitable distributors, arranging buyer meetings, or designing a small market test.

Solve it personally for one or two brands. Do not write platform software yet.

### Week Four: Try to Charge

The price can be modest, but the service cannot remain free forever.

Praise often proves only that someone is polite. Payment is much closer to evidence of real demand.

Then spend three months completing three to five cases.

If brands pay, distributors respond, and every completed case increases your data, process knowledge, trust, and probability of success, the business may begin to have the potential to become a platform.

If no one is willing to pay, that is not necessarily failure.

The result may have saved you months or even years of product development.

## Relationships Follow Multiplication, Not Addition

This brings me to the point I most want young people to understand.

Building connections matters, but relationships follow multiplication rather than addition.

The number of people you know, multiplied by the value you can create for them, is a better measure of your relationship capital.

If the value you create is zero, knowing more people still leaves the result close to zero.

So do not begin only by asking:

> How can I meet more investors?

> Who can introduce me to brand decision makers?

> How can I enter a more influential circle?

Ask first:

> Why should someone reply to me?

> What can I save for this person?

> What can I add for this person?

> What information, ability, or capacity to execute do I possess that this person needs now?

> If someone brings me into this circle, what value can I create for the people already in it?

A real network is not measured by the number of business cards you collect or the impressive names in your contacts.

It is measured by how many people trust you to deliver when an opportunity appears, how many think of you when they need help, and whether you can produce results when someone lends you their credibility.

## Your Earliest Product Is Credibility

Being young, lacking resources, and having no track record do not prevent you from becoming an entrepreneur.

They do mean that you cannot yet rely on past titles to earn trust. You must accumulate evidence through what you do now.

AI has indeed made building products easier than ever.

An undergraduate can now create in a few days what once required an entire team to prototype.

For that very reason, the ability to build something is no longer the scarcest resource.

What remains scarce is the ability to find a problem worth solving, reach real users, earn the trust of strangers, persuade the first customer to pay, deliver measurable results repeatedly, and turn one success into a repeatable process.

The first step in entrepreneurship is therefore not fundraising, recruiting engineers, applying to an accelerator, or incorporating a company.

It may not even be building a product.

The first step is to find one specific person with a serious problem and solve it once at the lowest reasonable cost.

Do it once, then do it again.

Do not rush to prove that you have a million dollar idea.

First prove that you can solve a problem worth paying for, for one specific person.

At the earliest stage, your real product is not a website, an app, or AI.

It is your credibility.

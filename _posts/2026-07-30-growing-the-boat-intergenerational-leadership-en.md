Over the past few days, I have kept returning to one question.

Does leadership suddenly emerge in a talented young person one day, or can it be cultivated gradually through an intentionally designed environment?

If we hope to see more future founders and integrators who can connect Taiwan and the United States, technology and education, community and industry, then the abilities we need to develop may go far beyond coding, presenting, or completing assigned tasks.

What matters even more is whether a young person can identify a problem, understand users, navigate uncertainty, coordinate different roles, and remain accountable for a shared outcome when there is no standard answer.

These abilities do not suddenly appear when someone launches a company, begins fundraising, or steps into a management role.

They leave traces.

And they can be developed intentionally, beginning in adolescence.

The recent Junior NATEA x UW Robotics, CS & AI Summer Program in Seattle gave me a glimpse of what this can look like.

## On the Surface, a Technology Camp; Underneath, a Leadership Experiment

The program was organized through a collaboration between Junior NATEA and partners at the University of Washington.

On the surface, a group of Taiwanese American high school students served as instructors and group leaders, guiding younger students through LEGO, coding, robotics, and AI.

Each day, the children built projects, tested programs, practiced loops and conditional logic, and developed their understanding of technology through teamwork, games, and presentations.

But to see it only as a technology camp would be to miss the more important design behind it.

What we wanted to cultivate was not simply whether the high school students could teach a lesson.

We wanted to see whether they could practice a deeper set of abilities—abilities much closer to the foundations of entrepreneurship and leadership:

How do we understand what participants actually need?

How do we respond when feedback is not what we hoped for?

How do we work with people who have different personalities and levels of experience?

How do we revise a process under constraints of time and resources?

How do we help a group move beyond merely completing tasks toward genuine shared commitment?

In that sense, this camp was both a technology learning experience and a youth version of entrepreneurial practice.

The high school students were not solving a simulated problem.

They were not completing an assignment in which following the teacher's instructions would automatically produce a good grade.

They were working with real children, real parents, real team dynamics, and new problems that could emerge every day.

## Real Users Do Not Always Follow the Designer's Expectations

As the camp progressed, different kinds of feedback began to surface.

Some children felt tired. Some thought certain activities were boring. Others felt uncomfortable with the way group members spoke or interacted.

Parents could often see emotions after the children returned home that were not immediately visible in the classroom, and they shared those observations with us.

If we interpret these voices only as complaints, a team can quickly become defensive.

But if we treat children and parents as real users, the same feedback becomes valuable information.

A product may enter the market and fail to resonate.

A process that appears reasonable on paper may not work in practice.

An activity that seems engaging to the designer may not feel engaging to the participant.

This is one of the most important lessons in both entrepreneurship and leadership.

My role was not to step in immediately and take over whenever a problem appeared.

As the program's faculty advisor, I was responsible for the overall educational design, on-site observation, communication with parents, and helping the high school team identify where the real problem was.

I first listened to the parents' concerns and tried to understand what was happening beneath the children's emotions.

Then I translated that information into questions the high school students could work with:

Where were children waiting too long?

Was the difficulty level mismatched with their age?

What was happening within the group dynamics?

How could the schedule, division of work, or communication be adjusted the next day?

The adult team did not provide every answer.

Instead, we gave the students enough support to propose their own changes and test them the following day.

When they saw that those changes led to real improvement, they gained something more meaningful than the feeling of simply having finished a lesson.

They began to understand:

I can learn from feedback.

A problem is not necessarily a rejection of me.

A team can improve through observation, communication, and revision.

## Before Young People Step Onto the Stage, Someone Has Already Built the Environment

By the end of the camp, the high school students felt that they had succeeded together.

In many ways, that sense of ownership was evidence that the educational design had worked.

They began to believe they could lead a group and complete something difficult.

That confidence is precious.

It is also an important beginning of leadership.

But no real project happens only because of the people standing at the front of the room.

Behind this program was a support system built by adults and first-generation Taiwanese Americans.

Yaohong Yang provided essential resources for the program. Professor Shih-Chieh Hsu at the University of Washington helped connect the program with campus and local resources. I was responsible for educational design, on-site guidance, parent communication, and team facilitation.

There were also parents willing to trust a group of high school students with their children.

There were community partners handling space, administration, and details that were easy to overlook.

And when problems emerged, there were adults willing to protect the learning environment rather than immediately question the young people's ability.

This support does not diminish what the high school students accomplished.

Quite the opposite.

Because others were willing to absorb part of the risk behind the scenes, the students could take on genuine responsibility in an environment that was relatively safe but still real enough to matter.

We often notice how a young person performs on stage.

We may not immediately see why a venue was willing to open its doors, why resources became available, why parents were willing to trust the team, or why people were still willing to give the group another opportunity when friction occurred.

Behind those opportunities were years of accumulated professionalism, relationships, credibility, and social trust.

When a young person is able to move forward smoothly, it does not mean their effort is less real.

It means someone was willing to open a door first.

It means their ability had a chance to become visible.

And before they had enough credibility of their own, they were allowed to begin by borrowing the credibility of an entire community.

## The Next Lesson in Leadership Is Understanding How You Were Supported

When young people take on a major responsibility for the first time, it is natural for them to notice what they themselves accomplished.

They prepared lessons, stood in front of a group, worked with students, solved problems, and invested a great deal of time and effort.

All of that deserves recognition.

But leadership has another lesson.

It is learning to see that personal effort was only one part of why the opportunity existed.

Someone else was maintaining the system in places that were not visible.

A mature leader does not believe that acknowledging support makes an achievement less meaningful.

Instead, they understand that anything worth building is usually sustained by many people.

Someone provides resources.

Someone opens the space.

Someone manages risk.

Someone protects relationships.

Someone executes at the front line.

And someone ensures that, when problems emerge, the team still has room to revise and try again.

When young people begin to see this full system, they move beyond being excellent executors.

They begin to become integrators—people who can design systems, mobilize resources, care for relationships, and create the conditions for others to succeed.

That is also a central part of entrepreneurship.

Entrepreneurship is not only about believing in one's idea.

Nor is it about attributing every success to individual ability.

Founders must know how to earn trust, build collaboration, bring different stakeholders into shared responsibility, and understand that every resource they use reflects another person's judgment and commitment.

## The First Generation's Accumulated Capital Is Becoming the Second Generation's Practice Ground

The story of the first generation of Taiwanese Americans in NATEA is itself a story of long-term accumulation.

Many left familiar environments and gradually established themselves within American technology, academia, industry, and professional communities.

What they accumulated was not only individual career success.

It also included institutional knowledge, cross-cultural experience, professional networks, and the credibility that leads others to trust them.

When this generation begins investing those resources in Junior NATEA, the meaning goes beyond organizing activities or maintaining cultural identity.

They are transforming decades of accumulated social capital into real environments where the second generation can participate, take responsibility, and practice leadership.

This is a deliberate form of intergenerational talent investment.

The first generation is not only telling the next generation to work hard and become successful.

They are creating a real stage on which young people can begin encountering users, coordinating teams, navigating setbacks, and learning how an idea becomes something that actually works.

This kind of environment cannot guarantee that every child will become an entrepreneur.

Nor can it manufacture the next unicorn.

But many of the foundational abilities associated with strong founders—problem awareness, user insight, rapid iteration, cross-cultural communication, resource integration, and the willingness to carry uncertainty—can indeed be cultivated earlier.

Education cannot guarantee a unicorn.

But it can intentionally build the soil in which founders, leaders, and integrators are more likely to grow.

## True Legacy Is More Than Helping Our Own Children Step Onto the Stage

Yet if the process ends with one generation helping only its own children, then the legacy remains incomplete.

A strong community is not defined only by producing a few outstanding young people.

It is not defined only by creating more opportunities for those already inside the circle.

The deeper question is whether those who benefit from support will eventually turn that support into an entry point for others.

Parents' networks, senior professionals' credibility, scholars' institutional access, and entrepreneurs' resources may initially create opportunities for a relatively small group.

But a healthy community should not allow those resources to circulate forever only among a few familiar families.

The more meaningful possibility is that each person who was once supported will, after becoming more capable, push the boundary of the circle outward.

That means creating space for young people who may not have the same family background, existing relationships, or early opportunities to prove themselves.

This does not require dismissing what the original community has built.

Nor does it mean opening every resource without judgment or structure.

It means allowing the achievements of the first generation to become more than a private advantage for a small group.

Over time, they can become part of a more open, more mobile, and more sustainable ecosystem of opportunity.

## Shared Prosperity Is Not Free Riding; It Is Building a Bigger Boat Together

If we imagine the community as a boat, shared prosperity does not mean that everyone simply climbs aboard and consumes what already exists.

Someone provides the first materials.

Someone designs the structure.

Someone helps determine the direction.

Someone steers through difficult waters.

Someone takes care of the people on board.

When young people first enter the boat, they can begin by learning, participating, and accepting responsibility.

They do not need to understand how the entire vessel was built from the beginning.

Nor do they need to possess the same skills as the first generation immediately.

But as they grow, they cannot remain passengers forever.

Eventually, they must begin asking:

What expertise can I contribute?

What barrier can I lower for the next group?

Am I willing to share the time, relationships, and credibility I have accumulated?

Can I help make this boat stronger, larger, and able to carry people from more varied backgrounds?

True legacy is not about ensuring that the next generation always has the best seat.

It is about helping them become people who are willing to build boats for others.

## In the Age of AI, the Rarest People May Be Those Who Help Others Succeed

As generative AI becomes increasingly capable of completing well-defined tasks, the abilities that remain scarce may no longer be limited to doing a task faster.

People who can only wait for clear specifications and follow instructions will become easier to replace with tools.

But the ability to identify problems amid ambiguity, understand different stakeholders, build trust, integrate resources, and design a system in which an entire team can function well will remain deeply valuable.

This is one of the questions I want to continue exploring after moving from Taiwan's higher education system to Seattle.

Can Taiwan and its overseas communities build a long-term model of talent development together?

Can the second generation of Taiwanese Americans become more than cultural connectors?

Can they become a new generation of integrators who connect Taiwan and the United States, technology and education, community and industry?

And once they gain greater ability and access, will they be willing to make the original circle larger and bring the next group in?

A five-day camp cannot answer every question.

But it allowed me to see that leadership does not have to be taught only through lectures, and entrepreneurial thinking does not have to wait until adulthood.

When one generation is willing to build a real environment in which the next generation can carry real responsibility; when adults are willing to support from behind without taking over the stage; and when young people, after succeeding, begin to understand how they were supported, legacy becomes more than a slogan.

It becomes a system that can continue operating.

The first generation passes its accumulated credibility to the second.

The second generation turns what it has learned into new opportunities.

Then it brings the next group on board.

We cannot guarantee which child will one day build a unicorn company.

But we can build a larger boat together—one that allows more promising young people to come aboard, learn to steer, and eventually turn back to help others.

The most valuable achievement of a community is not simply producing one successful person.

It is creating a culture in which every person who was once supported becomes willing to widen the circle, enlarge the boat, and pass forward the trust they once received.

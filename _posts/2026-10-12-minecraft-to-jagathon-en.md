In late September, I wrote about Stanley rebuilding Juanita Elementary School in Minecraft with his dad. At the time, we were reconsidering something that had seemed straightforward: the school fundraiser had a $125 goal per student, and we could simply donate that amount ourselves.

Instead, we decided to explore a few more steps with him. He could start with something he enjoyed, make a project, explain why he wanted to support his school, and practice inviting others to participate.

Father and son built the campus after school. We shared an exterior preview, followed by a video tour of the interior. Block by block, the world on his screen connected to the place he goes to every day.

On October 9, Stanley reached his first fundraising goal: $135 raised against a $125 target, or 108%. The two videos had also received a combined 88 views. The story then moved out of Minecraft and onto the school field.

It was Jagathon, the school’s fundraising run. Stanley was a runner; Joseph and I volunteered at a water station.

## His first fundraising project reached its goal

Over the past month, Stanley and his dad worked on the school after class each day, gradually building its exterior and interior. Through two YouTube videos, he invited friends and family to see the project and support his school.

Behind the $135 was a very real circle of people: $35 from me, $30 from Joseph, $10 from his grandmother in Kaohsiung, 黃松珠, $10 from community mom Aimee Hwang, and $25 each from our church small-group leader Jenn and our sister in faith, 陳尹.

Thank you to everyone who donated, and to everyone who opened the videos to see his work. Each contribution and each view brought encouragement to this little project.

I joked that we had experienced the “3Fs” sometimes associated with early startup support: Friends, Family, and Fools—the people willing to believe in you before there is much to show. Mom was his number-one fool!

That was our family’s playful analogy: these were donations to the school, not investments in a child’s business. Still, the feeling of having people believe in a project while it is taking shape was real. Beyond the dollar amount, his creation had reached actual people and received an actual response.

On the day of the event, he would take part with his feet as well.

## One lap, one mark, and eventually eighteen

After each lap, children returned to their class’s starting point, where parent volunteers marked their cards to record another completed lap. Then they headed out again.

Stanley ran eighteen laps!

Another parent volunteering at our water station proudly told us that his child had also been in first grade last year and had run nineteen laps. I loved that he still remembered. A number of laps around a school field had become a little piece of family history.

I was not interested in comparing eighteen with nineteen. What stayed with me was the child behind each number, returning to the starting point and choosing to go out again.

As an educator, I found the lap cards a thoughtful detail. “Keep trying” can be abstract for a first grader. Marks on a card are visible and countable. The run becomes a series of manageable tasks, with a chance to recognize progress at the end of each one.

The parent volunteers also become witnesses to that effort. A child returns, someone notices and records it, and the next visit adds another mark. Their actions leave a visible record. That is tangible feedback.

## An outstretched hand makes it easier to join

In the photos, colorful pennants line the field, a rainbow arch stands in the distance, and children wear their class colors. Stanley’s class wore black. Together they looked like a team, but once they started moving, each child had a different pace.

I especially liked how the adults encouraged children to enter the activity. Parents stood on both sides with their hands out, some bending down to greet passing runners. Children could exchange high-fives or smile as they went by. The whole setting seemed to offer a cheerful invitation: come join us!

Some parents volunteered, handing out water, marking cards, and helping the event run. Others came to cheer even though they had not signed up as volunteers. Everyone contributed in a different way, and the atmosphere felt relaxed and happy.

It made me think about the reassurance children sometimes need before trying something. When someone welcomes you at the beginning and responds as you pass, joining becomes something to look forward to. There is room to start, adjust, and continue at your own pace.

Later, it rained, and the children kept running. Some wore hoods; others were in short sleeves. The adults kept supporting them. What I remember is the little feet still moving forward, with people around them who stayed.

## A different view of support at the water station

At our station, small paper cups covered the table. Children arrived in groups, stopped for water, and returned to the activity.

Usually, we look after one child at home. That day, we were part of the support crew for many little runners. Their different expressions and rhythms made even this simple job interesting.

It also reminded me how practical support for learning can be: prepare what children need, help when they approach, and give them space to continue their own activity. The children ran; adults supplied water, recorded laps, and kept the event going. Their independent actions took place within that support.

This felt connected to our role in the Minecraft project. His dad helped bring the idea into a build, and I helped organize and share the work. On the day of the run, we took another position, supporting him and the other children from the water station.

## The PTA brings participation together, too

In my September article, I recorded the fundraising information we had received, including future fifth-grade camp, third-grade swimming lessons, and library books and learning materials. The PTA’s notice before Jagathon described supporting this year’s PTA programs and building a new Camp Savings Plan.

These purposes gave “supporting the school” a concrete meaning. They also helped me understand how the PTA connects families, the school, and the wider community.

National PTA’s fundraising guidance emphasizes that fundraising should serve its mission of supporting children and that the school community should understand how money is raised and spent. Behind a fundraising total is another question: what opportunities will these resources make possible for children?

For this event, families could share the online donation link or submit cash and checks. Those with employer matching contributions could provide confirmation so the matches counted toward their child’s prize credit. On the field, families contributed another resource: their time and presence.

Some donated, some organized, some volunteered, and some cheered. At this particular event, I could see how families and the community helped make part of school life possible together.

## Why involve the children?

Donating directly would have been a perfectly good way for us to support the school. We involved Stanley because we discovered ways to connect the activity to his own growing experience.

Building the Minecraft campus gave him questions to explore: What do I enjoy? What can I make? How can I help others understand a place I want to support?

Jagathon offered another way to participate: use his own feet and join his classmates in the activity.

These experiences make “I can contribute” more concrete. A child enjoys the activities and resources at school and can begin to recognize the people who organize and support them. He can also find a role of his own.

I suspect that is one reason parents enjoy encouraging their children at events like this. Seeing a child take part and finish something gives you a reason to feel proud. That pride can attach to small moments: joining the run, trying another lap, or sharing a project. The parent who remembered last year’s nineteen laps reminded me how long one day can stay with a family.

## It reminded me of the beginnings of entrepreneurship

I would not describe one school fundraiser as an explanation for American entrepreneurial culture. Families and schools approach these activities differently. Still, the experience reminded me of attitudes I have encountered around entrepreneurship here.

You identify a goal worth pursuing, explain it, offer something you can do, and invite others to support it. You may not have every resource at the beginning. Cooperation can help you move forward a little at a time.

The PTA’s event gave children a chance to see that process: people articulated a purpose, organized an activity, contributed different resources, and directed the results back to the school community.

Stanley’s Minecraft project was our family’s small exercise in something similar. Start with an interest, build a first version, share progress, receive responses, and keep working. Those experiences have something in common with the initiative, communication, and cooperation that entrepreneurship requires. For our first grader, the feeling we hope to leave with him is simple: I can bring an idea to life, take action for something I care about, and work with others to make it happen.

The happiness of the event mattered, too. When contributing is associated with laughter, friends, and high-fives, participation can become something a child wants to try again.

## From a school on the screen to the real campus

In late September, Stanley and his dad built his school in Minecraft, one block at a time. On October 9, he returned to the real campus and earned eighteen marks, one lap at a time.

First, he shared a place he cared about through a project. Then he joined his classmates in taking action.

Joseph and I learned along the way as well. We initially thought of fundraising as reaching a dollar amount. We came to see how much learning the process could hold: creating, communicating, accepting support, looking after yourself, and contributing to a shared community.

Thank you to the friends who supported Stanley’s school fundraiser, and to the PTA, teachers, and parents who made the day so welcoming.

Eighteen laps is Stanley’s record from this event. I also want to remember that each time he returned to the starting point, someone was there to add another mark and welcome him as he set out again.

Every Wednesday, elementary school lets out at 1:30 p.m. After picking up Stanley, we go home for a snack, then meet my nephew, who lives next door, at three to return books and borrow new ones at the library.

School pickup, a snack, meeting his cousin, and heading out together have gradually become part of our weekly routine.

I love this time. Browsing alongside Stanley gives me a glimpse of whatever has caught his attention lately. Some interests have been with him for a long time; others come from a small moment in that very day. Looking at the books he chooses feels like getting to know the world as he sees it.

## With a cousin alongside him, reading becomes something to share

Stanley has a cousin four years older living right next door. They can discuss which books to borrow and share the fun workshops they discover at the library. I often think how fortunate he is to have someone like that to grow up with.

His cousin is in fifth grade, where homework now includes more short essays that require organizing ideas and explaining them in writing. Stanley, in first grade, is asked to read one book aloud to his parents each day, building his vocabulary along the way.

They are at different stages of learning, yet they can visit the library together, each find something interesting, and bring their discoveries into their conversations.

Watching them reminds me that reading can be part of a shared life as well as time spent alone with a book. Having someone to go with and things to talk about adds something to look forward to.

His cousin does not need to be assigned the role of a little teacher. Choosing books together, talking, and sharing workshops already offer a valuable kind of companionship.

## How does one book a day become one after another?

Stanley enjoys reading English picture books aloud.

Although his daily assignment is one book, he often finishes it and reaches for another. “One book” does not seem to mark the end of reading for the day.

Reading one book is his teacher’s assignment; wanting to continue afterward is the response I see at home. That small distinction makes me want to pay closer attention.

The books contain vehicles, animals, and unfamiliar places he wants to investigate. In front of him are parents willing to listen. Both what he reads and whom he reads to connect with his own life. Reading aloud lets him share what is in those books with us.

His willingness to read a few more books cannot, by itself, tell me how much his reading ability has improved. But for now, he clearly enjoys continuing.

So alongside how many books he reads, I want to notice how he reads: What makes him want to keep going? Which books does he pick up again? What makes him pause to talk with us?

These details help me understand his developing relationship with reading more than a completion count alone could.

## He chooses the books himself, and he knows what he wants

Each time we visit the library, Stanley chooses around thirty books himself.

He has firm opinions about his choices. Something in each book interests him, and often I only discover where his attention has wandered when I see what we have brought home.

His interests have grown from machine tools, vehicles, and astronauts to all kinds of reptiles, with more subjects continually joining the mix. The books on the borrowing list cover many directions:

- **Tools and transportation:** trucks and construction vehicles such as scrapers.
- **Animals and animal research:** collies, robins, bald eagles, wild cats, saber-toothed cats, predators, and primatology.
- **Reptiles and amphibians:** copperheads, sea snakes, other snakes, frogs, and axolotls.
- **Nature and science:** gravity, mountains, and rivers.
- **The body and health:** skeletons, how injuries heal, food allergies, and keeping clean.
- **Countries, regions, and landmarks:** Belgium, Greece, Haiti, Indonesia, Iraq, Florida, the Eiffel Tower, and a book exploring the United States with Captain America.
- **People, history, and adventures:** Dr. Seuss, Theodore Roosevelt, immigration through Ellis Island, survival stories set during historical events, and adventures involving time travel and dinosaurs.
- **Holidays, seasons, and everyday feelings:** Groundhog Day, Halloween, spring, and picture books about mothers, love, and worry.

These are the categories related to children’s reading on our current borrowing list. They do not represent everything borrowed in a single visit today, or mean that every book has already been read.

Lining up the titles shows how far a child’s interests can range. One book is about construction vehicles, the next takes him to Greece, and another introduces the bones inside his body.

I may not know all his reasons for choosing each one, but his choices remind me that he is paying attention to the world in his own way. Choosing a book gives him a concrete opportunity to say, “I want to know about this.”

As adults, we can leave some room to notice those choices and listen to what he has to say.

## An axolotl plushie carries classroom curiosity into the library

There was a particularly sweet reason behind today’s choice of *Axolotls*.

His class held its stuffed animal party today, and one classmate brought an axolotl plushie. Stanley became interested in the animal. At the library that afternoon, he chose a book about it himself.

Just the day before, I had written about the celebration his class was preparing after filling its marble jar. Today, that celebration led to a reading subject I had not anticipated.

A classmate’s plushie introduced him to an animal he wanted to know more about. Our afternoon library visit gave him a chance to find a book. A small encounter in everyday life connected with a next step in exploring.

It made me think about what is available around a child once curiosity has been sparked.

Sometimes what is needed is a book that is easy to find. Sometimes it is someone willing to listen to questions. And sometimes it is simply a little room in the schedule to follow an interest further.

That afternoon, those conditions happened to come together. The axolotl went from a plushie in a classmate’s hands to a book we brought home.

## From construction vehicles to places around the world, I am learning to follow his interests

Another surprise today was how many books Stanley chose about countries, regions, and landmarks.

Belgium, Greece, Haiti, Indonesia, Iraq, Florida, and the Eiffel Tower: together, the titles look like a little travel itinerary he has put together himself.

I do not know exactly which pictures, names, or details attracted him, but I am happy to see him willing to bring unfamiliar places home to explore.

As a mother who has also spent many years working in education, I can easily see an interest and immediately think of ways to extend it: finding maps, organizing themes, designing activities. A whole learning plan can quickly begin to take shape.

But choosing books with Stanley also gives me practice in pausing first.

His interests can have room to remain as they are for a while. He can want to read about Iraq today and return to snakes tomorrow. He can keep reading about one subject or suddenly become drawn to another. I can browse alongside him and listen, then help find another resource when he wants more.

I want to become better at recognizing when my help enables him to keep exploring, and when leaving more space allows him to choose the direction.

## Two bags of books leave room for trying things and changing his mind

Stanley keeps choosing until two large shopping bags can barely hold everything. Only then is he satisfied and ready to go home.

Taking him to the library really does require a little upper-body strength along with time.

What I want to treasure, though, is his engagement as he chooses. Thirty books is a borrowing count. It cannot directly tell me how much he understands, and it does not need to become a reading schedule he must complete.

I hope the books we bring home can allow for different ways of reading. Some can be read again and again. Others can begin with looking at the pictures. And after browsing one, he may discover that it does not interest him very much right now.

Exploration includes trying things and changing one’s mind.

One thing I value about the public library is how easily it makes those attempts possible for our family. A child can borrow a book on an unfamiliar subject without first deciding whether it is worth buying. Whether he finishes it, browses it, or leaves it unread for now, he can choose again on our next visit.

Having access to many books and time to discover his preferences makes it easier for me to let Stanley choose.

Our weekly visits also give his curiosity a place to return to. Something he could not find this time, or a question that occurs to him after we get home, can be followed up the next week.

## Being there means helping his next step become possible

These Wednesday afternoons are gradually drawing my attention to some very practical questions.

What is Stanley curious about lately? Can he find books he wants to read? Is there someone to talk with? Does our daily life leave time for choosing, browsing, and sharing?

His cousin’s companionship gives him someone to share reading with. School activities introduce new things. The library provides books he can access. At home, we listen as he reads one after another.

Together, these everyday arrangements give his interests a chance to continue.

I treasure being beside him now, watching him choose books with such certainty and follow his curiosity to places I had not planned. Today it is axolotls and places around the world. I do not yet know what next week will bring.

When Wednesday comes, we will go home for a snack, then meet his cousin and head out together.

And we will have to remember those two very large shopping bags.

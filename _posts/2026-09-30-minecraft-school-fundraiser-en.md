These days, Stanley has something he wants to do after school: build Juanita Elementary School, the school he attends, in Minecraft with his dad.

The first version of the exterior is mostly finished. There are still plenty of details to add, but it already looks recognizable. We edited a short video of their progress and uploaded it to YouTube for everyone to have an early look.

[Watch the first video of Stanley’s Minecraft school](https://youtu.be/vGtPHg-wHT4)

The project began with a school fundraiser. As it has developed, I have started to see how much there is for him to learn along the way.

## We originally planned to donate the $125 ourselves

At the beginning of the school year, Stanley’s teacher introduced a fundraising activity with a goal of $125 per student. According to the information we received, the funds will support future fifth-grade camp, third-grade swimming lessons, and library books and learning materials.

Stanley attends a tuition-free public elementary school. We are also getting to know the voluntary activities through which families and the community can contribute to students’ learning resources.

My husband, Joseph, and I initially had a simple plan: donate the $125 ourselves. We had not planned to involve Stanley in fundraising, and we did not feel that raising this amount should be his responsibility.

Then a friend came over for lunch last week. We talked about how some American families treat school fundraising as an opportunity for everyday learning. Children can think about what they care about, what they are willing to work on, and how to explain what they are doing so that others can choose to support it.

That perspective was new to me. It encouraged us to reconsider a decision we thought we had already made.

## “What would you like to make to invite support for your school?”

So we asked Stanley what he wanted to do if he were to invite people to support his school.

His answer felt very much like him.

The school has merchandise connected to its upcoming fundraising run, but he wanted to make something of his own. He would build his school in Minecraft with his dad and create a campus people could explore. He also wanted to add a roller coaster and make a video introducing it, bringing some of his own imagination into the school.

He wanted to capture different views of the campus and turn them into digital thank-you cards for donors. He also imagined having a booth on the day of the school run, where other children could try his virtual campus. That is still an idea he hopes to pursue; we would need to check with the school before arranging it.

What I liked was that his proposal began with something familiar, something he enjoyed enough to spend time on.

Minecraft became a way to express his experience. He could rebuild a place he visits every day as a world that other people could see, understand, and perhaps explore with him.

## Building the idea with his dad, one piece at a time

Over the past few days, Stanley has been working on the campus with his dad after school. Little by little, the idea in his head has become a visible building on the screen.

So far, they have completed the first version of the exterior. We have selected six images from different angles for the digital thank-you cards and edited the short preview video.

The project is still in progress, but there is already something to share. I think that matters, too. He can see that a first version can take shape before every detail is finished, and that sharing progress helps people understand what he is working toward.

Next, he wants to build the inside of the school. I am also preparing to share the video and six exterior images with his teacher and ask whether there is a simple layout the school is comfortable sharing. Even a general reference could help him continue building the classrooms and common spaces.

## When it is finished, he will give the tour himself

Once the project is complete, Stanley will record the narration for the entire video himself. He will introduce his school from his own point of view.

I am looking forward to hearing it.

To an adult, a campus might be a floor plan, a row of classrooms, or a route for school pickup. What stands out to him? Which corners are the most interesting? What deserves a pause and a story? What has he noticed that we have walked past without seeing?

Choosing what to introduce and deciding the order of his tour will bring another kind of learning into the project: organizing his own experiences into a story someone else can follow.

Building the campus, choosing images, making cards, and recording narration all connect to real people. He is beginning to think about what others will see, what he wants to share, and how he can express his thanks.

## Learning to explain something he cares about

The fundraiser has a $125 goal, but in our family, that number does not need to become pressure on a child.

What matters more to me is that Stanley can begin with a real interest, invest time in it, make something, and explain the cause he hopes to support. Other people can freely decide whether to participate. He can practice respecting that choice and appreciating the support he receives.

Our friend’s perspective helped me see the everyday learning a fundraiser can offer: introducing an idea, interacting with people, and contributing to a shared goal.

This is an opportunity we encountered through this particular school activity. I would not assume that every American family approaches fundraising the same way. But it reminded me that learning can begin with a small moment in ordinary life.

Our child enjoys a game, and we can help him connect what he creates in it to the world around him. He wants to build a school, and together we can ask: How could he share it? How could it support a place he cares about?

## Looking for a friend who donated $25 anonymously

Today, we saw a $25 donation supporting Stanley’s school fundraiser. The system listed the donor as anonymous, so we do not know whom to thank.

We were deeply touched by this kindness.

Thank you for supporting the school and encouraging Stanley to keep bringing his ideas to life. For a child learning how to share his work with the world, this is a warm and meaningful response.

We would love to send you one of his digital thank-you cards. If you are reading this and feel comfortable doing so, please send me a private message or get in touch. You are welcome to remain publicly anonymous. We simply hope to have a way to send our thanks directly to you.

## A little campus that is still taking shape

The fundraiser closes on October 9, the day of the school fundraising run. We are inviting friends who would like to participate to give as little as $2, or choose another amount.

If you would like to support Stanley’s school, please contact me for the online donation link. After donating, please let me know so I can send you his digital thank-you card when it is ready.

For now, this Minecraft campus is still taking shape, one block at a time. The exterior is there, the interior is still ahead, and so is Stanley’s own narrated tour.

What I want to remember is the time father and son are spending together, and the moment a child begins to discover that something he loves making can carry a little kindness out into the world.
